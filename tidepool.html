Tidepool

Per-tile liquid levels from 0 to 255 that fall, spread and pool, drawn as pixel art. Hold the mouse to pour; right-click digs, soaks up and removes objects. Arrow keys walk and swim the explorer; Enter uses the bucket and D digs or places blocks; Shift-drag pans and the wheel zooms. Press / to find a tool, and Ctrl+Z to undo.

Materials

  • Sponge soaks up water and gives it back when a weight stands on it, or as steam when heated. Dirt soaks up a little water as wet soil, drips it into caves and sprouts plants. Porous rock lets liquid seep through.
  • Wool barely takes up heat (and burns); copper takes it up fast and carries power. A membrane lets only its chosen liquid through. Coral grows in warm salt water and bleaches when it's too hot. Fuse cord burns along its length, round corners, and sets off nitro.
  • Void ink deletes liquid; mimic becomes the first liquid it touches; coolant never freezes or boils and quenches lava; a magnet pulls ferrofluid into spikes; liquid light glows; fertiliser speeds plants up, greens stone with moss, and feeds algae.

Chemistry

  • Acid and alkali. Acid and lye poured into water dissolve into it and spread, weakening as they go. Hover over water to read its pH. Where acid and alkali meet they cancel out, warming the water and leaving salt.
  • Strength matters. Strong acid in water still eats dirt, stone and other blocks it touches, more slowly the weaker it is, and is used up doing it. Weak acid is harmless. Lye turns oil into soapy foam and kills slime.
  • Fizz. Acid on baking soda fizzes into carbon dioxide. It's heavier than air, so it sinks and pools in low places, puts out fires, and the explorer can't breathe in it. A little dissolves into water, making it faintly acidic.
  • Crystals. Still, saturated brine slowly grows glowing salt crystals on any surface, and fresh water dissolves them again.
  • Electrolysis. Power through brine, or a live metal or copper rod dipped in fresh water, splits the water into hydrogen and oxygen, two bubbles of hydrogen to one of oxygen. No water is made or lost, and the salt stays behind, so split brine gets saltier.
  • Smelting. Ore, found in veins deep underground, melts above 850° in lava, in molten metal or in a wood fire built around it (a boiler is far too cool). Two tiles in three become molten metal and the rest molten slag, which floats on the metal and sets into slag; water quenches it at once.

Fantasy liquids

  • Aether. Falls upward and pools on ceilings. It is lighter than every other liquid, so it bubbles up through them. It glows violet, sheds drifting motes, and crystallises lava into glowing crystal.
  • Quicksilver. Heavy, fast and opaque. It sinks through any other liquid and pushes it up instead of reacting, and it beads up rather than spreading thin. Pour it into a lake to watch the water lift.
  • Acid. Eats through dirt, stone, ice and honey blocks it touches, using itself up as it goes. Water dilutes it back to water. It fizzes and gives off a green glow.
  • Frost. A slow, glittering liquid that freezes any water it touches into ice. Thin puddles of it freeze solid on their own. Lava melts ice back into water.

Underground is dark. Sky light seeps in through openings, and lava, aether, acid, frost and crystal light the caves around them. Toggle it with L.

World, time and sound

  • The world is 512 × 128 tiles. The camera follows the explorer. Shift- or middle-drag pans it and stops following (J resumes), and the mouse wheel or Z zooms between 1× and 2×.
  • Sleeping chunks. The map is split into 16 × 16 tile chunks. The liquid update skips a chunk once nothing in or next to it has changed for a second, and anything that touches it wakes it again. Active chunks (B) outlines the awake ones. Once things settle, most of the world sleeps.
  • Day and night take three minutes. The sky is a smooth gradient; its light dims and turns blue at night, warms at dawn and dusk, and stars and the moon come out. Glowing liquids, fire and the explorer's lantern matter more after dark. The clock is in the status line.
  • Time of day (in Display & world) can hold the world at noon or midnight, or stop the clock where it is; back on day and night it carries on from there. Seasons keep turning either way.
  • Sound (M) is synthesized in the browser: splashes, sizzling steam, crackling fire, acid fizz, a chime when crystal forms, switch clicks and a pump hum. Sounds are quieter the further they are from the middle of the view, and pan left or right.
  • Each liquid sounds like itself when it splashes or is poured: water splashes, lava blorps, honey and mud gloop, acid hisses, quicksilver pings. Liquids in view make their own sounds too (lava pops, acid fizzes), and bubbles blub.
  • Music is a soft score made up as it plays: a slow pad and a sparse melody, in a major key by day and a quieter minor one at night, darker in the rain.
  • Looks. Distant hills and the cave walls scroll slower than the world. Clear water reflects what's above it and throws moving light on its bottom in sunlit shallows, and bubbles rise from gas, fizzing, boiling water, fish and the explorer underwater.

Fire and living liquids

  • Oil floats on water and shows a rainbow sheen. Above 150° it ignites, as long as it touches open air. Fire heats everything around it, so it spreads to more oil, wood, honeycomb and crates. Water puts out burning wood, but it sinks under burning oil instead of covering it, so an oil fire keeps burning on top of a lake. Smother it with dirt or stone, or let it burn out.
  • Wood (Y) burns away after a few seconds. Fire lights up the caves.
  • Slime creeps slowly and eats the dirt it touches, turning it into more slime, up to a limit. Heat, lava and acid kill it, frost freezes it, and bloomwater calms it into moss.
  • Bloomwater turns stone into glowing moss and sprouts glowcap mushrooms on dirt, using itself up as it goes. It also neutralises acid.
  • Pressure (O) is on: liquid pressing down on a full body comes out at that body's lowest open edge, so both arms of a U-bend, or two shafts joined by a tunnel, level out. Turn it off to save a little time.

Mixing

  • Dissolved contents. Water can carry salt, sugar, essence and ink. They flow with it and slowly spread into the water around them. Point at a tile to read what it holds.
  • Salt (S) is a powder that dissolves into water as brine. Brine is heavier, so it sinks under fresh water and lakes form layers. It freezes far colder than fresh water, frost can't freeze it, and boiling it away leaves salt behind.
  • Inks tint water red, yellow or blue. Where two colours meet they mix like paint: red and blue swirl into purple, blue and yellow into green.
  • Water dissolves honey into sugar; icy water (below 10°) sets it into honeycomb instead. Bloomwater dissolves into water as essence.
  • Brewing. Water with sugar and essence, heated past 80°, brews into glowing tonic. Tonic grows moss and glowcaps fast, and a dip wards the explorer against lava, acid and heat for ten seconds. Salt and essence chilled below 0° brew into frost.
  • Foam. A pump forcing oil into water (or water into oil), or water crashing onto oil, churns the two into foam. Foam floats on water and slowly separates back into oil on top of water.

Powders

  • Sand (A) and salt fall, slide off each other into slopes, and sink through liquids lighter than themselves. Sand floats on quicksilver.
  • Fine amounts. A powder tile holds an amount, like a liquid: boiling or drying brine leaves just the salt that was in it (a pinch from weak brine, nearly a tile from strong), syrup leaves sugar, and snow falls in thin layers. Layers settle onto each other into piles; only heaped powder slides into slopes; thin layers don't stop the explorer, and water dissolves powder a little at a time. Sugar caramelises above 180°.
  • Sand soaks up water into slow, sticky mud, which sinks under water. Heat or lava bakes mud into clay brick.
  • Sand touching lava melts into glass: a see-through block that lets light into caves. Acid can't eat it. The Glass tool places it directly.

Momentum

  • Moving liquid keeps moving. A broken dam surges across a tank, climbs the far wall and sloshes back before it settles. Thick liquids like honey and mud hardly slosh.
  • Pump outlets and springs jet their liquid sideways, falls splash outward where they land, blasts throw liquid out, and anything dropped into a pool sends out a wave. Fast water shows whitecaps.
  • Currents carry floating objects, waterfalls shove them under, and a drain at the bottom of a pool becomes a whirlpool that drags them round. Turn Momentum off for the old, calmer flow.

Weather and erosion

  • Weather (I). Open water evaporates into the air, more in warm, sunny, windy, dry weather. Rising or cooling air condenses into clouds, which drift on the wind and rain on what's under them, or snow where it's below freezing. Rain now gathers the air's moisture into clouds over the view, so in dry air it brings little rain.
  • Air gets colder with height, so wind pushed up a mountain rains on that side and the far side stays dry. Calm, cold nights leave fog in the valleys. Tall storm clouds over warm ground throw lightning, which sets wood alight, powers metal and sets off nitro.
  • The wind shifts every few minutes. It carries clouds, smoke and gas, ruffles open water, fans fires downwind and turns windmills. The status line shows the season, day and wind.
  • The open air is warm by day and freezing at night, and the year turns through four seasons over eight days. Lakes under the open sky freeze over on cold nights. Caves keep a steadier temperature that rises the deeper you go. Turn Weather off for still, mild skies.
  • Erosion. Fast water wears away dirt and sand and carries them off as brown silt, most of all where a waterfall lands. Where the water settles, the silt sinks and drops out as mud, and buried mud slowly packs back into dirt, so streams dig their own channels and pools.
  • Valleys and deltas. A river wears away the soil over it as well as beside and under it, and a bank it undercuts slumps in as mud and is washed away, so over time its channel opens into a valley. Where it reaches still water the silt sinks and settles, and a delta builds out from its mouth.
  • Groundwater. Rain and puddles soak into soil, which holds up to about a third of its volume in water. The water sinks and spreads until it gathers over the rock as a water table. Soaked soil seeps water out wherever it meets open space, so hillsides grow springs and a pit dug down into soaked ground fills up like a well.
  • Lake ice floats. Ice that freezes on water drops as the water under it drains, comes back up through water poured over it, and pieces break off a sheet's edge and drift with the current and the wind.

Biomes

  • Every world starts in grassland and runs through three to five other biomes, each with its own climate, ground and features: forest and jungle with trees, a swamp of silty pools and mud, desert dunes with the odd oasis, salt flats round a brine lake, badland mesas of clay and brick, tundra snow over permafrost, glacier peaks of ice, a coast with a tidal sea, and a volcano with a lava crater, a geyser and a gas vent.
  • Biomes have dry and wet spells: a drought warms the air so clouds dissolve and puddles dry fast; a wet spell cools it and draws moist air in. Humid biomes such as jungle and swamp gather moisture from the rest of the world's air, and dry ones lose theirs. The status line names the biome you're looking at.
  • Nothing comes from nowhere. Every drop of rain and flake of snow evaporated from somewhere first: lakes and puddles, drying mud and soil, and plants, which breathe out the water they drink. The tide fills the coast from the open sea beyond the edge and drains back into it.
  • Rivers. Forests, jungles and grasslands away from the start often have a river: it rises at the highest ground, runs down a channel on a clay bed and ends in a clay-lined pond with fish and a pike. Water that brims over the pond seeps back underground to the source, so a river flows without making or losing water; the sun evaporates some, and rain gives it back. Forests and jungles have moths, and trees slowly regrow the leaves they eat.
  • Deep down every world has water-filled aquifers (dig carefully), caverns with oil and quicksilver, and a magma sea at the very bottom.
  • New blocks: snow (melts into water, packs into ice), permafrost (thaws into mud), clay (bakes into brick); new machines: windmill, geyser and gas vent.

Creatures, plants and puzzles

  • Fish swim in water and breed in a pool together. Stranded, they flop towards water they can see and soon die; acid, poison, power, lava and hot water kill them. A new world has some in the cave lake.
  • Salamanders live in lava and glow. Out of it they wander, setting what they walk on alight, but water kills them and they can't stay cool for long.
  • Pike are big fish that hunt the small ones, which flee them. A pike breeds only after a good meal and starves without prey, then rots and feeds the algae the small fish eat, so a lake swings between them instead of filling up.
  • Moths flutter low over the ground and eat leaves. They breed while fed and die of hunger, frost, fire or a soaking, so winter thins them out. Fish leap out of the water to snap up moths flying low over it.
  • Slimelings hop about and bite the explorer. They soak up slime and split in two. Salt, acid, lava and power kill them.
  • Plants grow from a Seed on dirt, drinking water from around their roots (tonic makes them shoot up), and wilt in a drought. A grown plant with water regrows the leaves it loses and now and then drops a seed on open soil nearby, so plants spread along a riverbank.
  • Algae grows in water that gets light (daylight under open sky, or liquid light beside it). Plain water feeds only a faint green film; fertiliser feeds it into a thick bloom that turns the water green and suffocates fish. In the dark algae dies back, and in cold water too: it grows slowly below 15° and dies back below 8°, so open lakes bloom in summer and clear in winter. Fish graze algae and breed only while fed, so cave fish don’t multiply. Dead fish, slimelings and wilted plants rot into fertiliser, which feeds the algae again; dead algae rots too.
  • Puzzles. Pick a level from the Mode menu. Each one has a goal, a dashed goal region and only the tools it needs; some can be lost, so watch the goal bar. Sandbox brings back the open world.
  • Level editor (in the Mode menu). Build a level in the world with any tool, then give it a name, a goal (fill the goal area with a liquid, make blocks in it, get creatures or the explorer into it, or light lamps there), a way to lose if you like, and the tools players may use. Mark the goal area by dragging over it. Play test starts it from the world as it is; Back to editor returns. Copy level code gives a code anyone can play with Save & share › Load code.

Gases and nitro

  • Gases flow like thin liquids. Smoke rises from fires and thins out. Methane bubbles up from slime and collects under ceilings; it drifts away in open sky. Poison gas, from acid eating metal, is heavier than air and pools in hollows and on water. It hurts the explorer and withers moss.
  • Methane catches fire above 120° or against lava and burns in a flash that runs through the whole cloud.
  • Hydrogen is the lightest gas: it rises and escapes from the open sky. It catches above 100° and burns in a flash into hot steam, the water it was split from. Oxygen is a little heavier than air: it pools in hollows and slowly mixes away in the open. The explorer can breathe it, and fires burn hotter in it and use it up.
  • Gases don't carry, lift or shove the explorer or other bodies; they only matter where they hurt (poison gas, scalding steam). Oxygen sinks slowly, like the heavier gases.
  • Warm and cold liquids. Honey runs when warm and stiffens in the cold, oil thickens in the cold, and lava, molten metal and slag slow down as they cool towards setting.
  • Mixtures explode. Hydrogen or methane touching oxygen doesn't just burn when lit: it explodes like nitro, and the blast sets off the rest of the mixture.
  • Nitro (U) explodes above 150°, after a fall of six rows or more, or when live power touches it, so a lever and a metal line make a detonator. It sinks through water, where it's safe. A blast breaks blocks (not obsidian, metal or machines), throws liquid and objects outward, leaves smoke, and heats everything nearby, so one charge sets off the next.

Power

  • Conductors. Metal, stormwater and brine carry power from any switch or generator touching them to every machine they touch, so a pool can be wiring. Fresh water doesn't conduct. Live liquid crackles and glows, kills slime, and throws the explorer out.
  • Long lines lose power. Power fades as it travels: it reaches about 120 tiles of copper, 60 of metal or 40 of brine or stormwater from its source, and machines further out get none. Hover over a conductor to see how much is left. Wires made with the Wire tool don't fade.
  • Molten metal is heavy and white-hot. It sets into metal as it cools, or at once where water hits it, so you can pour your own wires.
  • Generators. A turbine spins while steam presses on it, and cools that steam back into rain. A water wheel turns while liquid flows past it. A boiler heats to 200° while powered: put one under a tank, a turbine above, and the turbine powers whatever it's wired to.
  • Solar panels make power in daylight under open sky (not under heavy cloud or at night), or underground beside a pool of liquid light.
  • Batteries charge while a powered line or a wire feeds them (a full charge lasts 20 s), and give the power back to the line and their wires once nothing else does, so a solar panel's lamp stays lit after dark.
  • Lamps light up only while powered. Motors run only while powered: a running motor drives every machine it touches, as if wired to it, and pushes liquid through itself towards its marked side (click it to turn it round). An electromagnet pulls ferrofluid and iron weights like a magnet block, but only while powered.
  • Coolant-fed boilers. A boiler touching coolant runs on the coolant's heat instead of its own: it boils while the coolant is above 200° and draws that heat off. Heat coolant on lava and pipe it to a boiler somewhere else.
  • Logic. AND, OR and NOT gates and counters take their inputs from wires and touching conductors, and switch whatever they're wired to. A counter turns on at every Nth pulse; click it to change N. Click a liquid sensor with a liquid picked to make it sense only that liquid.

Pipes

  • Pipes (N) are closed except at their ends. Liquid flows from the end with the higher surface to the lower end, and climbs into a tank that's already filling, so two tanks joined by a pipe level out.
  • A valve is a pipe tile that passes liquid only while it's powered, or after you click it open. A filter passes only the liquid that was picked when you placed or clicked it.
  • A separator splits foam back into oil and water and pulls salt, sugar, essence and ink out of the liquid it touches. The salt comes out as salt blocks beside it.

Switches and machines

  • Switches: a lever flips when clicked with the Lever tool, or when the explorer stands by it and presses Down. A pressure plate is on while an object or deep liquid rests on it. A liquid sensor is on while any side touches liquid at least half a tile deep. A timer is on for half of its cycle; click it to change the cycle. A temperature sensor is on while it's hotter than its setting, and a depth sensor while at least its setting's depth of liquid stands over it (gas doesn't count); click either to change the setting.
  • Pixels light up in their colour while powered (click one to change it), and don't pass power on, so a row of them wired one by one makes a sign or a counter.
  • Targets: a floodgate is solid when closed and passable when open. A spring pours its liquid without end (click it to switch to the liquid you last picked). A drain swallows liquid on all sides. Pumps can be targets too.
  • Wiring: with the Wire tool (K), click a switch, generator or logic gate, then click each target to link or unlink it. A linked target runs while any of its switches is on. Targets with no wire and no live conductor keep their own setting: springs, drains, boilers and separators always run, gates and valves toggle when clicked, and lamps, motors and electromagnets stay off.
  • The lever next to the explorer at the start runs the lake pump.

Heat

  • Every tile has a temperature that spreads to its neighbours. Rock and lava hold heat, open air sheds it, and flowing liquid carries its heat along.
  • Lava keeps itself near 1000° while it's deep. Thin films can't, and harden into basalt below 650°. Rock beside lava heats up and glows.
  • Water boils into steam above 100° and freezes below −5°. Steam rises, gathers under ceilings, cools and falls back as rain.
  • Ice melts above 40°, honey caramelises above 180°, acid boils off above 120°, and frost holds itself at −40°, chilling what's around it.
  • The Heat and Chill brushes change temperature directly. Heat view (H) colours every tile by temperature.

Pumps and floaters

  • Pumps. With the Pump tool, click once to place an inlet and again to place its outlet. A wire links the pair. The inlet drinks from its four neighbours and the outlet releases the same liquid on its top and sides, at up to 48/255 per tick. It only releases downward when its top and sides are walled in. Click either end to switch the pair on or off. The status light shows the colour of the liquid it's moving. Digging out one end removes both.
  • Buoyancy. Each tick a body measures how much of its box is under liquid, tile by tile, using the drawn height of each tile's liquid. It sinks with gravity g and is pushed up by g × Σ(area × liquid density) / (box area × body density). Drag depends on the liquid, and a small sine term makes floating bodies bob.
  • Currents. Level differences across a body push it sideways, so a pump outlet or a spill carries crates along.

Flow rules, in update order

  1. Each tile stores one liquid and an amount from 0 to 255. The grid updates bottom row first, alternating row direction each tick.
  2. React with touching liquids, then with touching blocks (acid eats, lava melts ice, thin frost freezes).
  3. Fall one tile in the liquid's gravity direction: down for most, up for aether. A denser liquid falling onto a lighter one swaps with it.
  4. Spread by averaging with lower side neighbours. Each liquid has its own minimum puddle size and its own tick rate, which sets how thick and slow it is.

Each liquid is one row in a property table: gravity, tick rate, puddle thresholds, density, glow and palette. A new liquid is mostly a new row plus its reactions.
